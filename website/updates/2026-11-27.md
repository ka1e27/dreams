# Trusses to hold the hulls together

Our bridge, the arch that joins the two hulls, is 3D printed. Printed plastic alone isn't stiff or strong enough to hold two hulls together in waves, so two aluminum trusses run inside it from one hull to the other, one near the front of the bridge and one near the back. A truss is a frame of triangles, like the steel under a railway bridge, that carries a lot of load for little weight. This week we worked out what shape ours should be.

![Our catamaran in 3D (SolidWorks): the pink bridge arches from one red hull to the other. The near hull has its lid on, with Dr. Eams on its side.](img/upd-2026-11-27-boat-1600.jpg)

## What the trusses have to do

Each truss is a flat plate cut from 1/4 inch (6.35 mm) 6061-T6 aluminum, curved like the bridge and 39 mm deep. Its two feet bolt to the hulls. We designed it for the loads a catamaran's bridge sees:

- **Twisting.** When one hull meets a wave before the other, that hull is pushed up while the other is pushed down. At our design load, each truss takes about 270 N (about 60 lb) up at one foot and down at the other.
- **Spreading or folding.** Waves and knocks push the hulls apart or together. We allowed 100 N (22 lb) pushing each hull in or out at each truss, about twice what we estimate a steep 20 cm wake can do.
- **Handling.** People will lift the boat by one hull, or by the bridge.

![The bridge from behind, arching from hull to hull](img/upd-2026-11-27-stern-1600.jpg)

We design every part to a safety factor of 3: it should take three times its worst expected load before the metal starts to bend for good. For this aluminum, that happens at a stress of 276 MPa (megapascals; 276 MPa is about 40,000 pounds per square inch).

## Letting the computer place the metal

Instead of guessing at a pattern, we used topology optimization: we gave a computer the truss's outline and its loads, and it removed material wherever the material was doing little work until only 40% of the plate was left.

![What the topology optimization kept: solid edges, diagonals at about 45° across the middle, small trusses in the curves and solid feet](img/upd-2026-11-27-optimizer-1600.jpg)

It kept both edges solid and filled the straight middle with diagonals at about 45°, a pattern called a Warren truss. When one hull meets a wave first, the whole middle of the truss is sheared the same way, and diagonals are the most efficient way to carry that. They work in tension or compression, so they handle the wave coming from either side.

## The design

We turned that into shapes a waterjet can cut. Ten diagonals, 6 mm wide, zig-zag between 6 mm edges, and every inside corner is rounded, because cracks start at sharp corners. The curved ends carry the most bending, so they keep their edges whole and lose only three 18 mm holes along their middle, where the metal carries the least. The legs and feet stay solid, because that's where the truss bolts to the hulls.

## Checking it

A computer stress check (finite element analysis) of the final shape under all of these loads found the highest stress is 66 MPa, about a quarter of what it takes to bend this aluminum for good. That's a safety factor of about 4, above our target of 3. Each truss weighs 243 g, 37% less than a solid plate (385 g).

![Stress in the truss, the highest of five load cases at each point](img/upd-2026-11-27-stress-1600.jpg)

## How it attaches

Each truss foot bolts down through the hull's lid into an aluminum bracket. The brackets sit flush in pockets in the top edge (the rim) of each hull, so the lid still closes flat and seals on its foam gasket, and the bolts clamp the truss, the lid and the bracket together.
